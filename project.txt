Vet your sources:	Read this short article about how to spot misinformation in a source.
Source 1: Sawka, M. N., Burke, L. M., Eichner, E. R., Maughan, R. J., Montain, S. J., & Stachenfeld, N. S. (2007). American College of Sports Medicine position stand: Exercise and fluid replacement. Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, 39(2), 377–390. https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31802ca597
What main points of information will you be using from this source? Bullet points are fine.
- Recommended sodium concentration (20–30 mEq/L) and potassium (2–5 mEq/L) for fluids during prolonged exercise.
- The actual physiological role of Na⁺ in maintaining plasma osmolality and preventing hyponatremia.
- When electrolyte replacement is genuinely needed (exercise >1 hour, heavy sweating) vs. when plain water is sufficient.
- Sweat composition data I can compare against drink labels.

How do you know that this source is credible:
This is the official position stand from the American College of Sports Medicine, peer-reviewed and published in Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise (one of the top sports-medicine journals). The lead authors are PhD/MD researchers at the U.S. Army Research Institute of Environmental Medicine and major universities. It's heavily cited (thousands of citations) and is the standard reference clinicians use for hydration guidelines.

Source 2: Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health. (n.d.). Sports drinks. The Nutrition Source. https://www.hsph.harvard.edu/nutritionsource/healthy-drinks/sports-drinks/
What main points of information will you be using from this source? Bullet points are fine.
- Most people, including casual athletes and kids, don't exercise long or hard enough to need the electrolytes or sugar in sports drinks.
- Added sugar content per bottle and how it stacks up against AHA daily limits.
- Public-health framing of how sports drinks contribute to childhood obesity and dental erosion.
- The mismatch between marketing claims and actual nutritional need — directly supports my S&S argument.

How do you know that this source is credible:
Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health is one of the most respected public-health institutions in the world. The Nutrition Source is reviewed by Harvard faculty nutritionists, cites primary peer-reviewed studies inline, has no advertising, and is non-commercial. It explicitly discloses authorship and review process.

Source 3: Committee on Nutrition and the Council on Sports Medicine and Fitness. (2011). Sports drinks and energy drinks for children and adolescents: Are they appropriate? Pediatrics, 127(6), 1182–1189. https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2011-0965
What main points of information will you be using from this source? Bullet points are fine.
- Direct statement from the American Academy of Pediatrics that routine consumption of sports drinks by children should be discouraged.
- Specific data on sugar content in popular sports drinks and links to obesity and dental erosion.
- Distinction between "sports drinks" (electrolytes + carbs) and "energy drinks" (stimulants), which is often blurred in marketing.
- Evidence base for my claim that kid-targeted sports-drink marketing has real public-health consequences.

How do you know that this source is credible:
This is an official clinical report from the American Academy of Pediatrics, published in Pediatrics (the AAP's flagship peer-reviewed journal). It was authored by two AAP committees (Nutrition + Sports Medicine), each composed of practicing pediatricians and researchers. AAP clinical reports go through formal peer and policy review before publication.
